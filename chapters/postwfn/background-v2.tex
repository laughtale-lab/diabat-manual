\section{Background}
The \texttt{postwfn} module offers analyses that require electronic-state wave functions rather than molecular orbitals alone. A \texttt{\$\$statepack} block supplies the orbitals and the selected electronic-state data, as described in Chapter~\ref{chap-interfaces}. The selected states may be adiabatic states from an external quantum chemistry calculation or quasi-diabatic states previously exported by \progname{}. The main analyses are based on the one-particle density matrix, the transition density matrix, or the difference density matrix. These quantities answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

\paragraph{One-particle density matrices.}
The one-particle density matrix describes the electronic population and coherence between orbitals in a specified electronic state. Its diagonalization gives natural orbitals and their occupation numbers. The occupations can help identify changes in electronic structure, including the importance of configurations beyond a simple closed-shell orbital picture. Suitable one-electron integrals also give the permanent electric dipole and other supported permanent multipole properties. Electron- and spin-density cube files provide spatial views of the same state-specific density information.

\paragraph{Transition density matrices.}
A transition density matrix connects a pair of electronic states. Its contraction with an appropriate one-electron integral gives an interstate property, such as the transition electric dipole moment. Singular-value decomposition of a transition density yields natural transition orbitals (NTOs), which pair orbitals associated with the removal and attachment aspects of the transition. The corresponding singular values indicate the relative contributions of different pairs. Transition-density cubes retain the sign of the transition density, whereas particle- and hole-density analyses provide complementary nonnegative spatial distributions. These outputs are useful when examining the orbital character of an excitation or comparing transitions between different pairs of states.

\paragraph{Difference density matrices.}
A difference density matrix is obtained by subtracting the density matrix of a selected reference state from that of a target state. Its positive and negative components describe where electronic density has been gained and lost relative to that reference. Natural difference orbitals and attachment and detachment densities provide orbital and spatial representations of these components. Unlike a transition density, a difference density does not require a nonzero transition matrix element between the two states. Its interpretation nevertheless depends on the reference state and on the compatibility of the orbital and AO representations used in the two calculations. If a task needs a specific loaded reference, select it with the corresponding \keyref{key-postwfn-refid}{refid} keyword.

Choose the analysis according to the quantity of interest. Use a density-matrix task to examine an individual state's occupations or permanent properties, a transition-density task to examine the relationship between two states, and a difference-density task to compare the electronic density of a target state with that of a reference. For the resulting cube files, begin with a moderate spatial grid and increase the resolution if the intended visualization requires it.
